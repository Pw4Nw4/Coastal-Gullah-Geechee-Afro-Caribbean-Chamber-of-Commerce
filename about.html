We exist to educate, empower and advocate for Gullah Geechee, Afro-Caribbean, minority-owned and women-owned businesses throughout the Georgia Coast and beyond.

Why we started

That thought is where the chamber began. Mahogany has filled a gap like this before: as a child, she was the one who needed a book bag, and there wasn't one, so she went back and started Blessings in a Book Bag, her nonprofit. Seventeen years later, she started over to fill this one.

She saw the gap on menus across Savannah, where Gullah Geechee food was called a Southern tradition, a Southern delicacy, a Southern favorite. “That's not what it is. It's more than that,” she says. “When it comes to shrimp and grits, that was created by Gullah Geechee people. And those people are my people.”

The chamber is here so the people who created this culture are seen in it, credited for it and able to build businesses on it. We didn't start it to be against anything. We started it to be for something, and to bring people together.

Culture is our foundation and commerce is the pathway forward. We operate by appointment and through community-based programs and events.

What is Gullah Geechee?

Gullah is the culture. Geechee is the people.

Gullah is the shrimp and grits you eat downtown and the red rice you find recipes for. It's the sweetgrass baskets hanging in the shops, the oral tradition of our storytellers, the dancing, and the praise houses that are still alive today. Geechee is the people: the times we connect, rejoice and celebrate, and the times we build an altar to remember who we are and where we come from.

The Gullah Geechee are descendants of West and Central Africans who were enslaved on the coastal plantations of the Carolinas, Georgia and Florida. On the Sea Islands and along the coast they kept a distinctive language, foodways, music, craft and spiritual traditions alive, and Congress designated the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor to recognize and help protect them.
